In 1978, computers were not personal possessions. A machine like the University of Texas at Austin’s PDP-10 was an enormously expensive institutional resource, accessed through terminals by many users and intended for research and other serious computing work. But a small group of programmers used it to create something surprising: a text-only multiplayer game. Building on an existing lineage of text-based space games, and in particular on an underground interest in Star Trek-inspired games, they turned the PDP-10’s underlying job system into an online virtual galaxy called DECWAR. In that galaxy, as many as eighteen players at separate terminals could simultaneously command their own starships, capture planets, build starbases, cause supernovas, battle the opposing faction, and communicate by subspace radio—all through typed commands.

DECWAR did not remain at UT. For $50, the university distributed its source code on magnetic tape to other universities, where the game became an underground hit. One of those tapes reached CompuServe, one of the first major commercial online services, where DECWAR received a commercial face-lift and a new identity as MegaWars, sustaining a paying audience for roughly a decade. DECWAR itself was never forgotten, but the code and history of its earliest forms nearly slipped out of reach. More recently, a group at UT Austin went looking for the surviving code. They found a CompuServe copy in the hands of a former UT employee. Years earlier, it had arrived in a mysterious email. Today, the group is restoring the historical code, producing a precise specification of the game, and building a new version using modern technology. Their investigation asks not only how DECWAR worked and changed, but why a world made almost entirely from text inspired its original players so deeply—and continues to inspire the people restoring, studying, and reinventing it today.

DECWAR matters because it reveals a path to online multiplayer games that ran not through arcades, home consoles, or commercial game studios, but through university mainframes, shared source code, and early commercial online services. Roughly fifteen years after MIT’s Spacewar! demonstrated a fully graphical game with motion and physics, DECWAR raises a striking question: how could a text-only game, played by typing commands into a terminal, create such an immersive and engaging environment? Its history brings together subjects that are often treated separately: the underground culture of Star Trek-inspired games, the resources and freedoms available in university computing centers, the emergence of multiplayer design, the informal circulation of software, and the conversion of that software into a commercial service. The book does not depend on claiming that DECWAR was the first of anything. Its significance lies in showing, in unusual detail, how the technical and institutional conditions for an online world came together—and how that world changed as its code passed from programmers to universities, commercial operators, players, and preservationists.

The book approaches DECWAR as both a historical subject and a working piece of software. It combines institutional records, surviving magnetic-tape files, source code, documentation, oral histories, player memories, comparisons among versions, and executable reconstruction. It also covers the reconstruction effort, which involved using AI to analyze the syntax and semantics of over 20,000 lines of FORTRAN and MACRO-10 assembly code. The restoration team’s game specification and modern implementation provide additional ways to test how DECWAR behaved and to distinguish its original design from later changes. Technical material will be introduced through concrete actions and discoveries: the job system through the arrival of multiple players, shared memory through changes inside the common galaxy, and reconstruction through the problems encountered while rebuilding the game. Terminal sessions, diagrams, annotated source excerpts, and version comparisons will allow readers without programming or vintage-computing experience to follow the evidence and understand why it matters.
