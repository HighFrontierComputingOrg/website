# MIT Press proposal strategy and draft language

## Recommendation

Submit this as an **exhaustively researched, object-centered history of DECWAR for MIT Press's Game Histories series**. Its organizing argument is convergence: a traveling family of text-based *Star Trek* games, university computing resources, time-sharing and remote networks, and hardware newly capable of coordinating many active jobs came together at UT in 1978. DECWAR’s decisive design move turned those jobs into player ships sharing one galaxy. The second half follows the code into institutional distribution, CompuServe and MegaWars, fragmentary provenance, and reconstruction at UT Austin.

The project is proposal-worthy now. The full submission is not quite ready now. Its decisive strengths are the recovered source environment, living reconstruction, first-person witnesses, regional history, and the authors' own routes into the story. Its main weaknesses are scope sprawl, an unfinished evidence/rights audit, inconsistent confidence in some blog claims, and the need to decide exactly who the author or authors are.

Because the project has been invited, follow the inviting editor's instructions rather than sending an inquiry. Confirm only the practical variables that the generic form leaves to individual editors: number and length of sample chapters, preferred file format, and whether the editor wants a bibliography or illustration sample. The proposal itself should identify Game Histories directly.

## Working identity

### Recommended title

**DECWAR: Eighteen Players, One Machine**

Why it works: it leads with the game and makes its technical and social achievement instantly legible. The title is specific, memorable, and source-supported: DECWAR defines eighteen player slots, divided into two teams, all running on one time-shared DEC-10. It avoids an unsupported “first” claim while setting up the book’s central reveal—the machine saw jobs while the players saw ships.

### Strong alternatives

- **Eighteen Ships: DECWAR and the Making of a Multiplayer World** — the most evocative alternative; the eighteen-slot limit is confirmed in the source even though the manual retains a stale ten-player description.
- **DECWAR: When Computer Jobs Became Players** — states the conceptual innovation directly but is slightly more technical.
- **DECWAR: From *Star Trek* to MegaWars** — captures lineage and commercial transformation but underplays university resources, networks, and reconstruction.
- **A Galaxy in Shared Memory: DECWAR and the Making of Multiplayer Play** — visually and technically evocative, but less immediate.

Retain **“We Share Our Time”** as a recurring period phrase inside the manuscript if permission and context allow. It neatly connects institutional time-sharing to social play, but it should not carry the book's title or dictate the chapter structure.

### One-sentence proposition

DECWAR emerged when a traveling text-game tradition met university-scale resources, remote access and early networks, and a machine just capable of coordinating many active jobs; by turning those jobs into ships, its creators made a real-time multiplayer world whose code later moved through CompuServe and MegaWars and is now being reconstructed at UT Austin.

### Thirty-second verbal pitch

In 1978, programmers at the University of Texas made a DEC-10 do something unexpected. The operating system thought it was running separate jobs; DECWAR turned those jobs into as many as eighteen starships fighting in one real-time galaxy. The book brings together the text-based *Star Trek* games, university resources, remote networks, and threshold hardware that made this possible, then follows the released code into CompuServe’s brand-cleansed MegaWars and back to UT Austin, where AET and DecwarOrg collaborators are reconstructing both the historical program and the environment that produced it.

## Publisher and series fit

The fit with MIT Press is substantive and specific.

- The [Game Histories](https://mitpress.mit.edu/series/game-histories/) series emphasizes critical historical study, multidisciplinary methods, historiographical care, and exhaustive research. This project joins archival records, oral history, code study, reconstruction, and close analysis of play around a single historical game.
- The series is edited by **Henry Lowood and Raiford Guins**. The proposal should demonstrate its historical method explicitly rather than relying on DECWAR's obscurity or age as proof of significance.
- [Noah Springer](https://mitpress.mit.edu/noah-springer/) is the relevant MIT Press acquisitions editor for Game Studies and New Media and handles Game Histories. His stated interest in deeply researched work with engaging storytelling fits the project's executable archive, participant voices, commercial transformation, and reconstruction method.
- The [History of Computing](https://mitpress.mit.edu/series/history-of-computing/) readership remains an important secondary market because DECWAR cannot be understood apart from TOPS-10, time-sharing, university infrastructure, and software practice.

The inviting editor is the correct editor. The proposal's primary identity should now be unequivocal: **a Game Histories book about DECWAR**, with history of computing, software studies, and Texas history as cross-markets. The test for every contextual passage is whether it helps explain how the game was made, played, circulated, interpreted, or preserved.

## Draft prospectus

### Brief description

In 1978, players at separate terminals on the University of Texas DEC-10 entered the same textual galaxy. Each commanded one of as many as eighteen ships, joined the Federation or Empire, fought over bases and planets, messaged teammates by subspace radio, and entered or left while the battle continued. To the operating system, each ship was a separate time-sharing job. To the players, those jobs were identities inside one real-time world.

*DECWAR: Eighteen Players, One Machine* explains how four developments converged to make that transformation possible. A widely copied family of text-based *Star Trek* games supplied a recognizable form. University computer centers supplied machines, terminals, compilers, staff knowledge, and a culture of after-hours experimentation. Regional remote-access systems and the ARPANET were changing expectations about where computing could be reached, even though DECWAR’s own relationship to those networks must be stated carefully. Finally, the DEC-10 and TOPS-10 offered hardware and operating-system mechanisms just capable of supporting separate jobs that interacted through shared memory. Robert Schneider, Jeff Potter, Bob Hysick, and other contributors turned those conditions into a multiplayer design.

The book then follows DECWAR beyond its first institution. UT released a source distribution package; copies traveled and changed; and an early CompuServe version was adapted and stripped of *Star Trek* branding as MegaWars. Rather than center accusations about the transfer, the book asks what changed when collaborative university software became a commercial online product. A 1995 email from an unidentified sender later preserved an early CompuServe-era source environment containing UT material. That layered code eventually returned to the university and became the basis for successive reconstruction work. Today, collaborators connected to UT Austin’s Arts and Entertainment Technologies program use emulation, replica hardware, decwar.js, and Project UTEXAS to work both forward toward access and backward toward the historical source distribution.

DECWAR broadens game history beyond commercial products and famous individual designers. It shows how a game can be made by the convergence of a software tradition, an institution, a networked moment, a platform, and a community—and how code changes meaning as it crosses from university resource to commercial service to historical evidence.

### Outstanding features

- **An executable primary source.** The authors can inspect, rebuild, run, and demonstrate the historical system rather than relying only on screenshots or recollection.
- **A four-thread convergence story.** Text-game lineage, university resources, networks and remote access, and threshold hardware become parts of one intelligible game history.
- **A precise architectural insight.** The operating system’s jobs become player ships; shared memory becomes the common galaxy. The book explains this through play rather than abstraction.
- **An underrepresented form and region.** It recovers a collective, noncommercial, text-based mainframe game made in Texas within histories still dominated by commercial products, famous designers, Silicon Valley, the Northeast, and PLATO.
- **First-person access.** The project includes creator testimony, staff recollections, player memories, preservation correspondence, photographs, and a near-contemporaneous account of the DEC-10 shutdown.
- **Technical detail in narrative form.** Shared memory, separate jobs, terminal speed, generated source, emulation, and containers are explained through scenes and player actions.
- **A transformation across institutions.** DECWAR moves from university source distribution to CompuServe and MegaWars, showing how platforms, branding, audiences, and business models reshape a game.
- **Reconstruction as historical method.** The unidentified 1995 sender, layered UT/CompuServe files, tape manifest, period tools, and repeatable builds make source archaeology part of the argument rather than an appendix.
- **A living public component.** Original/emulated and modern implementations support events, teaching, demonstrations, a companion site, and documentary media.
- **Contemporary relevance without presentism.** The book illuminates today's multiplayer platforms, online communities, software dependency, and reproducibility without pretending that 1978 was merely an incomplete version of the present.

## Detailed annotated table of contents

**Target:** approximately 95,000–100,000 words, including notes but excluding bibliography; prologue, eleven chapters in three parts, and epilogue. The allocations below total about 90,000 words of narrative, leaving approximately 5,000–10,000 words for notes. The prologue presents the finished multiplayer trick; Part I assembles the four converging threads; Part II explains design and play; Part III follows the code’s second lives and reconstruction.

### Prologue — Eighteen Players, One Machine (3,500 words)

A reconstructed session begins several jobs and turns them into ships. A scan, radio message, weapon delay, and attack make the architecture visible through action. The computer sees processes competing for time; the players see identities sharing a galaxy. The prologue asks how a university machine built for general computation became capable of supporting this world in 1978.

### Part I — Four Threads

#### 1. Star Trek on Paper (7,000 words)

From Mike Mayfield's BASIC game tradition to the UT FORTRAN *Star Trek* of Dave Matuszek and Paul Reynolds, command-driven space games move through listings, memory, and local adaptation. Printed source, handwritten changes, compilation, playtesting, and campus folklore reveal games as vernacular software rather than fixed products. The chapter is explicit about gaps: the immediate two-player predecessor WAR has an unknown author in the surviving DECWAR letter, and its precise relationship to other UT versions remains unresolved.

#### 2. Resources and Reach (8,500 words)

The university computer center becomes part of the invention. UT’s IBM and CDC systems, locally built interactive environment, staff expertise, compilers, storage, and terminals establish resources no individual creator could assemble. The Southwest Region Educational Computer Network and UT’s appearance on the 1977 ARPANET map show the changing geography of access. The chapter distinguishes regional service, remote terminals, and packet networking and does not claim that DECWAR itself ran over ARPANET without evidence.

#### 3. The Threshold Machine (7,500 words)

The DEC-10 arrives in 1975. TOPS-10 divides the machine among active jobs, while shared-memory mechanisms offer a path for those jobs to interact. Staff members, researchers, and students develop local tools and an after-hours culture around a machine operating near the edge of what an eighteen-slot real-time game would demand. The chapter makes the platform’s affordances and limits a source of design possibility, not background specification.

### Part II — Jobs Become Ships

#### 4. From WAR to DECWAR (8,000 words)

The four threads cross. Robert Schneider rewrites WAR on the CDC and begins its transfer. Jeff Potter and Bob Hysick then turn it into DECWAR during late nights around a coveted 9,600-baud terminal. Hysick writes the low-level assembler core while Potter develops FORTRAN components; the university’s distribution letter credits the redesign while preserving uncertainties about other contributors. Their decisive move is to let each ship run as a separate job while all ships act on shared state.

#### 5. Eighteen Players (9,000 words)

A reconstructed session supplies the book’s central close reading. Eighteen player slots divide into two nine-ship teams; bases, planets, the automated Romulan, black holes, weapons, repairs, movement delays, entry, exit, and subspace radio turn a textual grid into a shared tactical world. Commands and timing are explained through player decisions rather than a catalog of features. The manual’s stale ten-player introduction becomes a small historiographical lesson: running code can correct the document meant to explain it.

#### 6. The Galaxy's Machinery (8,000 words)

The source distribution becomes a tour of late-1970s game production. Separate TOPS-10 jobs communicate through shared memory. MACRO-10 and FORTRAN divide the work; TECO generates declarations and common blocks; RUNOFF produces documentation; build commentary records dependency and sequence. Diagrams, short excerpts, and a reconstructed build show how platform constraints became pacing, mechanics, and capacity—and why this game cannot be separated cleanly from its operating system.

#### 7. Playing Together (8,000 words)

Players make the programmed world social. Radio groups, gags, calls for help, team imbalance, command abbreviations, physical-time delays, skill, and the gripe system shape behavior. Oral histories from UT, San Marcos, Kentucky, and other sites recover local play cultures and the changes players and maintainers requested. Comparison with PLATO games, Maze War, Empire, MUD1, and other contemporaries locates DECWAR among early multiplayer worlds without unsupported priority claims.

#### 8. From DECWAR to MegaWars (8,000 words)

UT packages the source for distribution, and copies travel to other systems. At CompuServe, an early version retains much of DECWAR before being adapted and stripped of *Star Trek* branding as MegaWars. The exact transfer is treated carefully but does not drive the chapter. Its subject is transformation: what changes when university software becomes a branded commercial service for paying network customers?

### Part III — Second Lives

#### 9. The Source Trail Folds Back (6,500 words)

Harris Newman searches Usenet and emulators for historical DECWAR material. An unidentified correspondent sends an early CompuServe-era source environment around 1995, but the original message is no longer available. The copy preserves a crucial junction: UT files and structure embedded inside the commercial branch. Its eventual return to UT, later public availability, and Merlyn Cousins’s work making it run under standard emulation show how preservation can keep code while losing a clean account of its movement.

#### 10. Working Backward (8,000 words)

Project UTEXAS begins with the layered CompuServe-era files and works backward toward the UT source distribution. The tape manifest, build notes, TECO generators, period FORTRAN and MACRO-10 tools, SIMH, containers, and PiDP-10 reveal dependencies and changes that static reading misses. The chapter treats execution as historical method: each successful build helps distinguish UT structure, commercial additions, later repairs, and platform assumptions.

#### 11. One Galaxy Again (6,500 words)

The story reaches UT Austin’s Arts and Entertainment Technologies program. Eric Freeman and Mk Haley work with Noah Smith and other DecwarOrg collaborators to present historical and modern approaches side by side. decwar.js makes play immediately accessible; Project UTEXAS restores the historical workshop. The Fall 2025 event brings Freeman and Smith's own player histories together with students, collaborators, replica hardware, source, and modern interfaces.

### Epilogue — The Machine Sees Jobs (2,500 words)

A repeatable build starts several jobs. Players enter as ships and immediately change one another’s world. The book returns to its central image: what looks like workload management from inside the operating system becomes multiplayer culture at the terminals.

## Apparatus

The book should include:

- approximately **45–60 figures**, mostly black-and-white with a color insert or color throughout only if the economics permit;
- period photographs of machines, staff, terminal rooms, the 1982 shutdown, and distribution/recovery artifacts;
- screenshots or terminal captures from clearly labeled historical and modern implementations;
- new diagrams of the UT computing/network environment, DECWAR's separate-job/shared-memory architecture, the source-tape toolchain, game message flow, and preservation chain;
- short, legible source-code excerpts rather than decorative full pages of listing;
- a selective chronology, glossary, cast of people/institutions, notes, bibliography, and source/provenance note;
- a methodological appendix or online essay explaining reconstruction as historical method;
- a companion website containing a playable or demonstrable implementation as rights permit, source links, extended oral-history excerpts, build documentation, timeline, figure supplements, and teaching material.

The apparatus should remain subordinate to the narrative. A reader should not need PDP-10 knowledge, programming experience, or advanced mathematics.

## Audience

### Primary audience

- readers of serious game history, game studies, and media history;
- game historians, software-studies and media-archaeology scholars, and digital-game preservation professionals;
- game designers, developers, and programmers interested in how early multiplayer systems actually worked;
- museum, archive, and vintage-computing communities interested in executable games and historically situated play.

### Secondary audience

- general readers of narrative technology history and online-community history;

### Level

Descriptive and analytically rigorous, with technical concepts explained visually and through action. No mathematical or programming prerequisite. Notes and appendices should support specialist verification without interrupting the general reader.

## Market considerations

The book gives readers four things they cannot get from a general game history: an explanation of why several historical conditions converged to make DECWAR possible, a playable early multiplayer world examined at source-code depth, the transformation of university software into a commercial network product, and reconstruction used to distinguish historical layers in working code.

Relevant organizations and channels include:

- Digital Games Research Association, Foundations of Digital Games, Society for Cinema and Media Studies, game-history programs and symposia, and game-development educators;
- Game Developers Conference, IGDA, game-studies and game-preservation communities, and practitioner podcasts/newsletters;
- Strong National Museum of Play, Video Game History Foundation, Stanford's game-preservation community, Computer History Museum, and other collecting institutions;
- Vintage Computer Federation, Living Computers alumni communities, DEC/PDP-10 groups, SIMH and PiDP communities;
- Society for the History of Technology, SIGCIS, Association of Internet Researchers, IEEE History Center, ACM History Committee, and digital-preservation networks;
- Texas State Historical Association, Travis County Historical Commission, Briscoe Center, Texas Exes, UT departments and alumni groups, Texas technology organizations, and regional museums;
- readers and educators reached through the authors' existing O'Reilly, UT, software, design, and event networks.

### Market estimate

Market numbers for crossover scholarly books are inherently uncertain. A defensible planning estimate is:

- **core global addressable audience:** 25,000–50,000 readers across game history, game development, computing history, software preservation, and vintage-computing communities;
- **institutional/course market:** 1,500–3,000 plausible academic, public, technical, and museum library/course buyers over the book's life;
- **working English-language lifetime sales range:** 4,000–10,000 copies, with upside if the documentary, playable companion, events, reviews, or course adoption reach a broader technology audience.

Label these as estimates, not market research. The final proposal should add mailing-list sizes, comparable-title sales if the press can share them, event attendance, site traffic, newsletter/social reach, and realistic course-adoption targets.
